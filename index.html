Does El Niño Bring Rain to California?

Every time a strong El Niño shows up, Californians hear the same forecast: get ready for a wet winter. We looked at 65 years of climate data to see how often that's true.

Adam Hamadene, Dawai Yu, Emiliano Franco, Justin James · DSC 106, UC San Diego

Along the equator, the Pacific's trade winds normally push warm surface water west toward Indonesia. Every few years they weaken and the warm water drifts back east toward the Americas: that's El Niño. When they strengthen instead, the eastern Pacific gets even colder: that's La Niña. Their strength is measured by the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI): how many degrees warmer or colder than average the central Pacific is.

About the data

We use the CMIP6 AMIP experiment from NCAR's CESM2 climate model (run r1i1p1f1, 1950–2014), read from the public Google Cloud bucket gs://cmip6. In an AMIP run the model is given the real, observed ocean temperatures month by month, so every El Niño and La Niña happens in the year it really did. The model then simulates how the atmosphere responds. That means the ocean temperatures are observations, while the rainfall is the model's simulated response to them, not rain-gauge measurements.

Data transformations

  • Ocean temperature map: color is the difference from the 1981–2010 average for the same calendar month. Hovering shows the actual temperature (that average plus the difference).
  • Rainfall map: color is the month's rain as a percent of the 1981–2010 average for that month, on a log scale so that half the usual rain and twice the usual rain look equally strong. Places that are normally almost dry that month (under 0.3 mm/day) are left blank, since a percentage there isn't meaningful.
  • ONI and El Niño / La Niña labels: the ONI is the 3-month average temperature difference over 5°S–5°N, 170°W–120°W. Following NOAA, a month is El Niño (La Niña) when the ONI stays at or above +0.5 °C (at or below −0.5 °C) for at least 5 months in a row.
  • California winter rain: rainfall averaged over the model grid cells inside California's outline and added up over the November–March wet season, shown as a percent of the average 1981–82 to 2009–10 winter. Each winter's El Niño strength is its December–February average ONI.
  • Map grid: the model's ~1° grid was averaged into ~3.8° × 5° cells to keep the files small enough for a web page, then smoothly interpolated between cell centers for display.
  • Limitations: the data ends in 2014, so it misses the 2015–16 and 2023–24 El Niños. The rainfall comes from a single model run, so part of it is random simulated weather rather than an El Niño signal.

Writeup: Design Rationale

Our visualization explores whether El Niño brings more rain to California by allowing readers to compare El Niño and La Niña conditions with California’s simulated winter rainfall from 1950–2014. We combined a historical timeline with an interactive map so readers can identify events over time and examine the geographic conditions associated with a selected month.

Visual Encodings

The timeline uses bars as marks: horizontal position encodes time, while vertical position encodes the direction and magnitude of the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI). The zero baseline allows readers to compare values along a common scale using the length of the bars. Bars above zero represent warmer-than-average conditions, while bars below zero represent colder-than-average conditions.

We changed the plot’s original colors to a darker blue and red to improve discriminability between El Niño and La Niña conditions. Using blue for cold and red for warm is an intuitive association. Color distinguishes the climate categories, while position and length communicate magnitude. We added the rainfall plot directly below the El Niño strength graph and aligned the two along a shared time axis. This reduces the cognitive load of finding the same period between two separate graphs, allowing readers to compare corresponding periods easily. Similarly, the 100% reference line provides a common baseline for comparing wetter-than-normal and drier-than-normal winters.

The map uses geographic position to show where conditions occur and a diverging color scale to represent anomalies in ocean temperature. Its date corresponds to the time selected on the timeline. Blue and red distinguish the two deviations from the average: colder and warmer, respectively. The light midpoint represents values close to zero, maintaining the same color associations used in the timeline. California is highlighted because it is the central focus of our question, while major geographic locations are labeled for reference. The rainfall map uses a similar approach, with darker orange-brown encoding less rainfall than normal and darker blue encoding more rainfall than normal.

Interactive Design

The timeline and map are coordinated views: selecting a month in the timeline updates the geographic display. Hover tooltips provide precise values without crowding the display, and arrow keys allow readers to navigate changing conditions one month at a time. In addition, we added a dropdown menu containing selected El Niño and La Niña peaks. Selecting an event jumps to that period.

Design Process

Our development process involved preparing the data, building the timeline and map, connecting their interactions, and refining the presentation.
